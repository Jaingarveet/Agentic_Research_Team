
*****************INTRO:*****************

The global Small Modular Reactor (SMR) landscape is at a turning point, driven by a handful of leading developers in North America, Europe, and Asia and shaped by distinct licensing regimes, design maturities, and government support models. This report offers a comparative, cross‑regional analysis of the foremost designs, deployment timelines, cost trajectories, licensing progress, safety features, and investment drivers, with particular attention to how pre‑licensing activities, design certification pathways, and multi‑unit licensing influence near‑term deployment, site readiness, and supply‑chain commitments. Drawing on regulator‑facing analyses (including Part 53–style licensing concepts, NRC/ecfr frameworks, and EU/CAN regulators’ discussions), as well as developer perspectives and policy syntheses, the introduction situates how differences in standards, interfaces between modules, containment and safety‑system integration, and the regulatory posture on accident‑tolerant fuels translate into indicative capital, operating costs, and levelized cost of energy for utilities pursuing near‑term projects.
The analysis then foregrounds the levers most likely to accelerate deployment without compromising safety—design standardization, harmonized pre‑licensing frameworks, streamlined or joint multi‑unit licensing pathways, and assured, cross‑border supply chains—while acknowledging adoption barriers such as siting constraints, grid readiness, public acceptance, and waste management considerations. By integrating regulator‑driven governance with developer and investor perspectives, the report provides a structured, actionable view of risks, decisions, and potential pathways for policymakers and utilities navigating the global SMR opportunities and challenges.

*****************BODY:*****************

The global Small Modular Reactor (SMR) landscape is increasingly defined by a triad of forces in North America, Europe, and Asia: (1) regulator-led, Part 53–style licensing concepts and design certification pathways that are being refined to accommodate multi-unit deployments; (2) pre-licensing and design maturity activities that aim to reduce the time-to-licensing delta once a design is matured; and (3) multi-unit licensing pathways that could unlock serial module deployment and a more predictable supply chain. Across regions, these dimensions intersect with siting, grid readiness, waste management, and public acceptance, creating a complex but navigable set of decisions for utilities and policymakers. The near-term deployment window is therefore less a question of whether SMRs can be licensed and built, and more a question of how standardization, pre-licensing discipline, and cross-border regulatory cooperation can be operationalized to compress schedules, control costs, and assure safety at scale [Federal Register — Risk-Informed, Technology-Inclusive Regulatory Framework for Advanced Reactors; ecfr Title 10 CFR Part 53; Energy.gov Strategic Framework for SMR Deployment] [Federal Register; ecfr; Energy.gov].

1) Regulatory context, design certification, pre-licensing, and multi-unit licensing: shaping near-term deployment timelines and capital allocation

- Pre-licensing activities and design standardization. Pre-licensing workstreams—encompassing definition of design maturity, safety-by-design concepts, and interfaces between modules—are the primary vehicle through which developers and regulators narrow uncertainties before formal licensing submissions. International and North American analyses consistently flag pre-licensing as a principal determinant of downstream schedule risk, with regulator-facing syntheses emphasizing standardization and early engagement as a pathway to expedient licensing and predictable site readiness [OECD-NEA SMRs briefing (2019); SNETP European SMR Pre-Partnership Licensing Report; World Nuclear Association CORDEL report on facilitating intl licensing of SMRs] [OECD-NEA SMRs briefing (2019); SNETP; CORDEL]. In the U.S., pre-licensing discourse centers on how Part 53–style frameworks will govern early design discussions, accident-tolerant fuels debates, and the degree to which a design can be “frozen” before a formal design certification submission [NRC ML2613; ecfr Part 53; Sidley Environmental Health & Safety brief] [NRC ML2613; ecfr Part 53; Sidley]. The result is a strong alignment push toward harmonized safety cases and common design evaluation criteria to minimize rework and duplication across jurisdictions [World Nuclear News on European regulator collaboration; CORDEL report] [World Nuclear News; CORDEL].

- Design certification pathway and Part 53 dynamics. The design certification pathway for modular LWR SMR designs depends on regulatory constructs that can accommodate multi-unit serial approvals while maintaining safety rigor. In the United States, the NRC has publicly described how a modular SMR design would navigate a design certification track, with an emphasis on safety-by-design integration and interfaces among modules, while considering the practicalities of a multi-unit configuration [NuScale “How SMRs gain design approval in the U.S.”; Morgan Lewis licensing analyses] [NuScale; Morgan Lewis]. The final Part 53 framework—now publicly articulated in Federal Register materials and codified in ecfr—establishes a technology-inclusive approach focused on risk-informed pathways, with explicit emphasis on how multi-unit configurations and cross-border considerations may be treated within joint-review constructs [Federal Register; ecfr Part 53] [Federal Register; ecfr]. The practical implication for project developers is a closer alignment of design maturity milestones with regulator expectations, potentially compressing the design certification phase when standardized interfaces and common QA frameworks are in place [NRC; Morgan Lewis; Sidley] [NRC; Morgan Lewis; Sidley].

- Multi-unit licensing and serial approvals. In parallel with design certification, multi-unit licensing concepts—allowing serial approvals for multiple modules or reactor units at a single site or within a given holding company—are increasingly foregrounded by regulators and state coalitions as a mechanism to reduce repetitive licensing cycles and to synchronize supply chain engagement with site readiness. The Wyoming interim committee briefing, regulator-facing analyses, and regulator-drafted materials emphasize that future licensing regimes could enable streamlined serial approvals or a package approach for multi-unit deployments, provided interfaces, containment integration, and safety systems stay harmonized across units [Wyoming Interim Committee briefing; World Nuclear News; Sidley] [Wyoming NRCLicensing briefing; World Nuclear News; Sidley]. The regulatory community, as reflected in Part 53 discussions and related guidance, envisions a pathway in which joint reviews and modular licensing constructs can deliver time savings for utilities while preserving safety margins [Federal Register; ecfr Part 53; World Nuclear News] [Federal Register; ecfr; World Nuclear News].

- Interaction with safety systems, containment interfaces, and accident-tolerant fuels. A core residual uncertainty shaping near-term deployment is how design interfaces—between modules, containment boundaries, and safety-system architecture—will be standardized and validated in licensing reviews. The regulator-facing literature and design maturity analyses stress that clear, harmonized interfaces are essential to avoid bespoke approaches for each applicant and to enable scalable manufacturing and site siting [CORDEL; World Nuclear News; MDPI SMR analysis] [CORDEL; World Nuclear News; MDPI]. The regulatory posture toward accident-tolerant fuels (ATF) remains a live variable, with safety-case structures needing to accommodate ATF datapoints without creating licensing bottlenecks. The Part 53 regime contemplates technology inclusivity conditioned on rigorous safety assessments, implying that the pace of ATF adoption will hinge on regulator comfort with the risk-profile changes ATF may introduce [Federal Register; Sidley; MDPI] [Federal Register; Sidley; MDPI].

- Economic and cost-integration implications. The combination of pre-licensing standardization, design maturation, and the possibility of multi-unit licensing translates into meaningful implications for CAPEX, OPEX, and LCOE. Regulators and analysts emphasize that standardized designs and harmonized pre-licensing activities reduce schedule risk and lead to more predictable supply chains, which in turn lowers risk premiums and financing costs. This logic is echoed in policy streams and licensing analyses that link standardized designs, joint reviews, and predictable procurement to improved project economics, even as initial capital intensity remains higher than incumbent baseload plants due to the need for factory-grade manufacturing, containment integration, and safety system redundancy [OECD-NEA SMRs; World Nuclear News; Morgan Lewis; Energy.gov] [OECD-NEA; World Nuclear News; Morgan Lewis; Energy.gov].

2) Principal residual uncertainties and friction points: design standardization, module interfaces, containment/safety-system integration, and the regulatory posture on accident-tolerant fuels

- Standardization of designs. A central friction point is whether SMR designs can be standardized to the degree that a single licensing pathway can be applied across regions and customers. The literature consistently identifies design standardization as the most potent accelerator for deployment because it underpins modular manufacturing, reduces licensing duplication, and enables economies of scale in supply chains. The CORDEL and SNETP bodies emphasize standardized design evaluation criteria and common safety-case templates as prerequisites for international licensing harmony and export readiness [CORDEL; SNETP] [CORDEL; SNETP]. The EU and other regulator-driven efforts illustrate that even when designs are mature, divergent national interpretations of standards can slow down approvals unless joint-review mechanisms or mutual recognition arrangements are established [World Nuclear News; CORDEL] [World Nuclear News; CORDEL].

- Interfaces between modules and containment integration. Interfaces—mechanical, electrical, control, and containment boundaries—are a persistent scheduling risk if left under-specified in early licensing discussions. The design-maturity literature underscores that well-defined interfaces enable predictable factory assembly and site integration, while poorly specified interfaces risk rework and testing delays during construction and commissioning [World Nuclear News; MDPI] [World Nuclear News; MDPI]. The Rolls-Royce E3S Instrumentation & Control work, while primarily technical, signals the criticality of integration standards for safety systems across modular units, pointing to a need for consistent I&C architectures and QA across the fleet [GDA Rolls-Royce SMR I&C Case; Rolls-Royce document] [Rolls-Royce E3S I&C]. 

- Containment and safety-system integration. The movement toward multi-unit deployments increases the importance of integrated containment concepts that can be uniformly validated across units. Regulator-facing analyses stress that containment interfaces and safety-system integration must be captured in early Safety Cases and must withstand cross-border regulatory scrutiny, particularly if joint-review mechanisms are to be used for accelerated licensing [World Nuclear News; CORDEL; MDPI] [World Nuclear News; CORDEL; MDPI]. 

- Accident-tolerant fuels (ATF) posture. The regulatory stance on ATF is still evolving in many jurisdictions. The finality of Part 53 and safety-case approaches means ATF will be evaluated within the broader risk envelope of each design, with additional data and testing requirements potentially adding schedule pressure if regulators require extensive ATF demonstrations before approval. The regulator-focused literature indicates ATF could influence licensing timelines, depending on the strength and clarity of safety-case arguments and the availability of robust regulatory precedents [Federal Register; Sidley; MDPI] [Federal Register; Sidley; MDPI].

- Site readiness and grid interconnection. Beyond the design itself, siting constraints—geology, seismic risk, water availability, population density, and grid readiness—shape deployment sequencing. Regulators and policy analyses consistently highlight the need for harmonized site-permitting pre-qualification and grid-interconnection pre-certification to avoid standstill in the build-out stage. EU strategy documents emphasize that cross-border governance and pre-licensing harmonization are essential to align deployment momentum with grid and market readiness [EU Strategy; Energy.gov; CORDEL] [Energy.ec.europa.eu; Energy.gov; CORDEL].

3) Indicative cost trajectories: CAPEX, OPEX, and LCOE under plausible scenarios

- Base-case framing. In the near term, CAPEX per unit is driven by containerized module manufacturing, containment integration, safety-system redundancy, and the cost of maintaining a high level of redundancy and safety culture across units. The literature suggests that standardized designs and early pre-licensing alignment can reduce schedule risk, which lowers financing costs and can improve the overall LCOE by narrowing the dispersion of risk premiums. This is reflected in regulator-facing analyses and policy syntheses that connect standardized design maturity and joint reviews with favorable financing signals [OECD-NEA; Energy.gov; Morgan Lewis; CORDEL] [OECD-NEA; Energy.gov; Morgan Lewis; CORDEL].

- Scenario-based ranges. Given current regulatory trajectories, three scenarios offer a structured way to frame CAPEX/OPEX/LCOE implications:

  - Optimistic scenario (strong standardization, rapid pre-licensing, multi-unit licensing in lockstep, robust cross-border supply chains). Under this scenario, the licensing timeline compresses relative to traditional, bespoke licensing paths; the supply chain becomes highly repeatable and cost-competitive; and the LCOE improves through reduced risk premiums and higher capacity factors once modules are deployed in series. The literature supports this outcome whenever joint-review mechanisms are in place and where ATF and containment interfaces are consensus standards from day one [World Nuclear News; SNETP; CORDEL] [World Nuclear News; SNETP; CORDEL]. 

  - Central scenario (moderate standardization, phased pre-licensing, staged multi-unit licensing with some regional variation). This outcome reflects a pragmatic path where some regional divergences in standards persist, but shared safety-case templates and QA frameworks yield meaningful schedule and cost improvements relative to bespoke licensing. The trajectory aligns with regulator-facing expectations about Part 53 enabling a technology-inclusive framework while leaving room for national adaptations [Federal Register; ecfr Part 53; Sidley] [Federal Register; ecfr; Sidley].

  - Pessimistic scenario (limited standardization, fragmented pre-licensing, serial approvals hindered by cross-border differences and export-control fragmentation). In this scenario, CAPEX remains elevated due to bespoke licensing, more conservative risk premiums, and slower supply-chain ramp-up. The literature warns that if joint-review pilots do not deliver timely convergence on core design and QA standards, schedule slippage and higher financing costs could ensue [World Nuclear News; CORDEL; MDPI] [World Nuclear News; CORDEL; MDPI].

- Sensitivity drivers. The most consequential drivers of cost outcomes are (1) the pace and success of standardization (design maturity taxonomy, Safety-by-Design templates, and QA requirements), (2) the ability to implement multi-unit licensing without rework, (3) the robustness and predictability of cross-border supply chains, and (4) the regulator’s willingness to accept ATF data without protracted validation. The literature consistently identifies these as the levers that most strongly shape CAPEX/OPEX/LCOE and project risk profiles [SNETP; CORDEL; MDPI; Energy.gov; Federal Register] [SNETP; CORDEL; MDPI; Energy.gov; Federal Register].

4) Levers to accelerate deployment without compromising safety: priorities and trade-offs

- Design standardization. Recommendation: pursue a single, regulator-accepted common design maturity taxonomy and safety-case templates across markets, enabling serial manufacturing and cross-border procurement. This aligns with CORDEL and SNETP assessments and is reinforced by regulatory strategy discussions in the EU and North America that emphasize standardization to unlock export-readiness and supply-chain scale [CORDEL; SNETP; World Nuclear News] [CORDEL; SNETP; World Nuclear News].

- Harmonized pre-licensing framework. Recommendation: establish a harmonized pre-licensing pathway that allows simultaneous, regulator-led discussions across jurisdictions using shared safety-case constructs and testing requirements. This leverages the Part 53–style, technology-inclusive posture described in Federal Register/ecfr and in practitioner analyses, reducing duplication of effort while ensuring safety is preserved [Federal Register; ecfr Part 53; Sidley] [Federal Register; ecfr; Sidley].

- Streamlined serial multi-unit licensing. Recommendation: implement streamlined, serial approvals within a coordinated framework that certifies a design for multi-unit deployment rather than unit-by-unit licensing. This is squarely within the policy imagination of multi-unit licensing discussions and aligns with regulator-facing briefs and the Wyoleg briefing on NRCLicensing concepts [Wyoleg briefing; World Nuclear News; Sidley] [Wyoleg; World Nuclear News; Sidley].

- Assured supply chains and cross-border procurement. Recommendation: incentivize cross-border supplier qualification, standard QA documentation, and shared cross-border testing to reduce vendor risk and accelerate module fabrication and integration. This approach aligns with the literature on cross-border licensure and supplier-readiness, and it is a recurring theme in EU harmonization discussions and CORDEL's export-readiness framing [CORDEL; World Nuclear News; CORDEL] [CORDEL; World Nuclear News].

- Regulator-led, joint-review governance. Recommendation: establish formal joint-review mechanisms with regulator-led coordination across jurisdictions to deliver faster, shared safety judgments while maintaining national sovereignty over final licensing decisions. The regulator-facing literature (NRC Part 53 framework; World Nuclear News on cross-border regulator cooperation; MDPI regulatory synthesis) consistently points to joint reviews as the most scalable route to faster approvals without safety compromise [NRC ML2613; World Nuclear News; MDPI] [NRC ML2613; World Nuclear News; MDPI].

5) Policy and process reforms: what yields the largest gains?

- Establish international harmonization bodies with binding but reversible pilot outcomes. The literature supports establishing cross-border governance that can deliver mutual recognition of safety cases within defined pilots, using joint-review templates and standardized QA criteria. This approach would dramatically reduce duplication of licensing and testing across markets and could unlock cross-border supply chains [World Nuclear News; CORDEL; SNETP] [World Nuclear News; CORDEL; SNETP].

- Codify a clear, regulator-led fast-track for select designs. The Part 53 framework concepts and regulator-facing analyses indicate that a well-defined fast-track regime, supported by consistent design maturity thresholds and shared safety-case architecture, could dramatically shorten timelines for certain designs without impairing safety. This would require precise decision gates and performance-based triggers to maintain safety discipline while enabling acceleration [Federal Register; ecfr Part 53; Morgan Lewis; Sidley] [Federal Register; ecfr; Morgan Lewis; Sidley].

- Expand financing instruments tied to regulatory milestones. Regulators and policy analysts emphasize the critical role of financing instruments (credit guarantees, risk-sharing schemes) tied to joint-review milestones and standardized QA results. These tools would reduce perceived build risk, lower capex through lower discount rates, and accelerate market uptake by improving bankability of SMR projects [Energy.gov; ANS/NRC coverage; CORDEL] [Energy.gov; ANS; CORDEL].

- Governance reforms for cross-border data and safety culture. To sustain safety across borders, governance reforms should include data-sharing protocols, safety-culture metrics, inspection harmonization, and confidential reporting channels—all aligned with IAEA and CNSC/NRC-style governance frameworks—so that cross-border licensing can rely on robust, verifiable safety data and aligned inspection practices [IAEA materials cited in World Nuclear News; CNSC Four Pillars; NRC/Part 53 literature] [World Nuclear News; CNSC Four Pillars; NRC Part 53]. 

6) Near-term roadmap for a utility considering a near-term SMR project: milestones, triggers, and risk-adjusted financial projections (0–5 years and 5–10 years)

- Governance and foundational steps (0–12 months)
  - Establish a regional cross-border licensing task force under a regulator-led joint-review concept, with a standing chair and rotating regional representatives. Initiate shared design maturity taxonomy, common safety-case templates, and standardized QA frameworks. This aligns with Part 53–style planning and joint-review precedents in regulator literature and practitioner analyses [Federal Register; ecfr; World Nuclear News] [Federal Register; ecfr; World Nuclear News].
  - Map a pilot portfolio: select 1–2 SMR designs with clear modular interfaces for a cross-border joint-review trial; define site-readiness prerequisites, grid-interconnection pre-qualification, and permitting checklists. Use Wyoleg and regulator-materials to inform site permitting and NRCLicensing pathways [Wyoleg; World Nuclear News] [Wyoleg; World Nuclear News].
  - Construct a financing playbook anchored in milestone-based guarantees and risk-sharing arrangements tied to joint-review gates; solicit private capital with a government-backed credit enhancement framework to de-risk cross-border procurement, in line with Energy.gov and CORDEL financing discussions [Energy.gov; CORDEL] [Energy.gov; CORDEL].

- Early-stage execution (12–36 months)
  - Convene a formal pilot of joint reviews on the selected designs, with interim approvals for design maturity milestones and a defined go/no-go gating process. The objective is to complete cross-border design safety-case validation within a 12–18 month window for the pilot, documenting lessons learned and refining the joint-review templates [NRC; World Nuclear News; MDPI] [NRC ML2613; World Nuclear News; MDPI].
  - Initiate cross-border supplier qualification and QA documentation standardization for selected modules; begin multinational procurement arrangements for critical components, leveraging standardized interfaces to unlock scale economies. This follows the cross-border supply-chain emphasis in CORDEL and EU harmonization roadmaps [CORDEL; EU strategy] [CORDEL; EU Strategy].

- Scaling and maturation (36–60 months)
  - If pilot success indicators are achieved—time-to-approval reductions, robust joint-review acceptance of safety cases, cross-border vendor qualification completion—scale the approach to additional SMR designs and other sites, with broader cross-border procurement and financing tools. Expand the pool of permitted designs and sites under the harmonized framework [World Nuclear News; MDPI] [World Nuclear News; MDPI].
  - If pilot outcomes diverge, execute a staged remediation plan: revise joint-review templates, tighten safety-case requirements, adjust risk-sharing terms, or pause cross-border approvals to focus on national readiness and design maturation until confidence returns.

- Risk-adjusted financial implications for the utility
  - 0–5 years: Higher initial CAPEX due to the incorporation of cross-border standards, early-stage vendor qualification, and site-readiness work. However, the efficiency gains from harmonized pre-licensing and joint reviews are expected to reduce the total project duration and reduce discount-rate risk, potentially lowering levelized cost of energy (LCOE) relative to bespoke licensing trajectories. These dynamics are reflected in regulator-focused analyses and the financing-oriented policy literature [Federal Register; Energy.gov; CORDEL; World Nuclear News; Morgan Lewis] [Federal Register; Energy.gov; CORDEL; World Nuclear News; Morgan Lewis].
  - 5–10 years: With successful pilot scaling, LCOE could converge toward a lower band as cross-border supply chains mature, joint-review credibility grows, and financing terms improve due to reduced project risk. The literature consistently signals that multi-unit licensing and standardized QA, when achieved, yield material improvements in project economics over time [CORDEL; MDPI; Energy.gov] [CORDEL; MDPI; Energy.gov].

- Practical governance language: charter perspective
  - A fast-track governance charter would specify initiation by national regulators, with a rotating chair, defined triggers for joint review based on design maturity and cross-border risk cues, and a delineation of responsibilities between national regulators and an international coordinating body (with escalation pathways). It would also define go/no-go decision gates, joint-review timelines (target 12–18 months for pilot), and dispute resolution procedures. These principles are consistent with regulator-facing guidance and joint-review exemplars in World Nuclear News and NRC-era documents, and they map to the governance constructs seen in CORDEL and SNETP harmonization literature.

7) Integrating regulator-facing analyses with developer perspectives to form a coherent pre-licensing, design-certification, and multi-unit licensing strategy

- Convergence points
  - Agreement on the centrality of pre-licensing standardization and joint-review pathways to reduce duplication and accelerate approvals [OECD-NEA; World Nuclear News; CORDEL] [OECD-NEA; World Nuclear News; CORDEL].
  - Recognition that Part 53–style licensing concepts are designed to accommodate technology-inclusive reviews, with multi-unit configurations requiring careful interfaces and containment integration managed through standardized safety-case constructs [Federal Register; ecfr Part 53; Morgan Lewis; Sidley] [Federal Register; ecfr; Morgan Lewis; Sidley].
  - Shared expectation that ATF and other safety innovations will be evaluated within the evolving regulatory framework, but that a mature, safety-conscious approach to ATF data and licensing remains essential to maintain safety discipline while enabling acceleration [Federal Register; MDPI; Sidley] [Federal Register; MDPI; Sidley].
  - The EU and CORDEL’s emphasis on standardized QA and comprehensive design maturity taxonomy corroborate the regulator-trader view that a globally harmonized approach to design evaluation will unlock cross-border procurement and scale manufacturing [CORDEL; EU Strategy; World Nuclear News] [CORDEL; EU Strategy; World Nuclear News].

- Points of divergence
  - The pace and extent of mutual recognition and joint-regulator reviews may vary by jurisdiction, with some regulators preferring more cautious, staged alignment and others pursuing more aggressive recognition. This is reflected in practitioner analyses and EU-specific licensing discourse, which emphasize sequencing and governance design to avoid regulatory overreach while achieving speed [World Nuclear News; Morgan Lewis; ANS coverage] [World Nuclear News; Morgan Lewis; ANS].
  - The handling of ATF, fuel-cycle technology, and advanced reactor elements beyond SMR LWR designs may hinge on national risk appetites and export-control regimes, affecting schedule and financial planning for international deployments [Federal Register; Sidley; NANO Nuclear commentary] [Federal Register; Sidley; NANO commentary].

- Practical near-term actions for utilities
  - Align internal project governance with regulator-led joint-review principles; begin pre-licensing activities that map to standardized safety-case templates; establish cross-border supplier qualification and QA processes early; pursue a serial licensing plan rather than unit-by-unit licensing where feasible; and engage with export-control authorities early to minimize approvals friction for cross-border procurement [NRC; World Nuclear News; CORDEL; SNETP] [NRC; World Nuclear News; CORDEL; SNETP].

- Conclusion
  The near-term trajectory for SMR deployment hinges on translating regulator-facing, Part 53–style licensing concepts into practical, cross-border, standardized, and joint-review-enabled processes that utilities can actually absorb. The literature consistently identifies pre-licensing standardization, harmonized templates, and joint-regulator pathways as the core levers to compress schedules and lower risk, while preserving safety integrity. The remaining uncertainties—design interfaces, containment integration, ATF postures, and siting constraints—are tractable if addressed through a deliberate governance framework that codifies minimum harmonization elements and enforces disciplined risk management across the entire lifecycle. A structured, 3-year roadmap with regulator-led joint-review pilots, standardized QA, and cross-border supplier qualification can deliver measurable acceleration while sustaining the safety discipline that regulators and developers alike deem non-negotiable
